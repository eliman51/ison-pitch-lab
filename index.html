    How to practice this way, and at work
    • Hear the lock. When your hum and the tone are a little apart you hear a slow wobble, a "wah-wah" called beating. The closer you get, the slower it wobbles; when it stops, you are in tune. Hum rather than sing words: it is much easier to hear.
    • Slide slowly. If you hear beating, move your pitch very slowly up or down. If the wobble gets faster, go the other way.
    • Feel it too. In a loud shop you may hear yourself mostly inside your head. Notice the buzz in your face and chest when you lock in; that feeling becomes your memory of the note.
    • Safety first. Keep your hearing protection. Use protective headphones or isolating earbuds with sound, and never turn the tone up to compete with a saw. Pause before any cut that needs your full attention: one press on the headphone button.
    • Short and often. Your voice learns best in regular sessions of 10 to 30 minutes with breaks, not one long one. Sip water.
    • Keep the screen on. The app keeps it awake. Put the phone in your pocket in Pocket mode. If the screen locks, the sound stops and the session pauses; open the app and tap Resume.
    • The order. Start with Unit 0 until locking onto home feels natural, then 1, 2 and 3. Each unit shows a time goal; the level rises as your minutes grow. Use the Note explorer any time, and come back to the screen lessons now and then to see your real scores.

    Byzantine scale workout

    A complete warm-up and practice on the Byzantine scale, built on your home note. It moves from straw and hum work, to the two half steps and the wide step, to the open voice, to patterns, to the whole scale over the ison and in chant tuning, then a phrase and the scale from memory. It gets a little faster and stricter every few workouts.

    Today's routine

      Why it is built this way
      • Straw and hum first. Semi-occluded singing (straw, lip trill, hum) makes the folds vibrate with less effort; a few minutes of straw work measurably eased voices in a 2013 study (Guzman et al.), and it is the standard opener in voice science (Titze).
      • Small before wide. The scale is learned the way Byzantine theory teaches it: by tetrachords (four-note halves), and inside them interval by interval, so the half steps and the wide step are each solid before the full octave.
      • Middle voice before range. Everything starts on your home note and widens gradually, low effort first.
      • Patterns. Groups of three and broken thirds make you hear every corner of the scale, not just the run up and down.
      • Over the ison. Singing against a held tonic exposes small tuning errors as a slow wobble, which is why chanters and string players practice with a drone.
      • From memory last. Taking the guide away tests the inner ear, after the scale is warm.

            Challenge

            A session works through lessons in order from the one you pick. Sessions of 10 minutes or more open with a gentle straw or lip-trill warm-up, and 15 minutes or more end with a cool-down. Older lessons come back as review, weakest first. If the unit runs out before your time does, you get extra practice on its weakest lessons. A lesson is complete at 70 or better. Each unit ends with a test that touches every lesson's hardest exercise: score 90 to master the unit. Each unit also has a practice-time goal based on about 20 minutes a day, 5 days a week; every minute you actually sing counts toward goals and your rank, so extra practice moves you along faster. Exercises with an ison or a second voice work best with headphones. Everything stays open, so you can go back or jump ahead.
